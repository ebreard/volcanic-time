Hearing the catalogue

Each eruption record plays one drum or cymbal hit. VEI determines which instrument plays and which quiet piano note accompanies it. Higher VEI gives a lower, longer piano note. Unknown VEI gives only a drum-rim tap, with no piano.

Longitude sets the balance between the left and right speakers. Less precise start dates make piano notes begin more gently.

Recorded start dates set the timing. Different historical periods are sped up by different amounts. No backing beat or repeating melody is added; repeated sounds alone do not indicate a volcanic cycle.

      ABOUT THE DATA

      A record of observations, not a record of every eruption

      This visualization uses confirmed Holocene eruptions published by the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program. Older records are much less complete, and their dates are often known only to a year or range. The app uses a dated Holocene eruption export, not the live weekly activity feed. Twelve records in that export are dated before 9,700 BCE, the start of the Holocene, and are left out.

      Understanding VEI

      The Volcanic Explosivity Index (Newhall & Self, 1982) is a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 8, primarily based on erupted bulk tephra volume: ash, pumice, lapilli and volcanic bombs. Plume height, qualitative descriptions and other observations help assign an index when volume estimates are incomplete.

      8≥1012>25 kmMega-colossal

      Plume-height ranges overlap: these are supporting criteria, not strict independent cutoffs. Duration, stratospheric injection and qualitative observations can also inform an assignment; a recorded VEI does not guarantee a measured volume.

      Volume, not total magma output

      VEI is not simply log10 of total erupted volume. Thresholds increase tenfold from VEI 2 upward, but VEI 1 spans 104–106 m³. The VEI 8 threshold is 1012 m³, or 1,000 km³, of bulk tephra, not dense-rock equivalent (DRE). Bulk volume includes pore space; conversion to DRE requires density assumptions. Effusive lava output is not represented well by VEI.

      Size is not eruption rate

      A short, high-rate eruption and a prolonged, lower-rate eruption can share a VEI. Its mixed criteria describe explosive eruption size but do not isolate intensity. Pyle's magnitude, M = log10(erupted mass in kg) − 7, measures mass, while intensity measures mass eruption rate (kg/s). These complementary measures separate how much material erupted from how quickly it erupted.

      VEI alone is not a measure of local danger, fatalities or climate impact. Low-VEI eruptions can still be hazardous. In this catalog, ? means unknown, not VEI 0; no value is inferred for missing records.

      Newhall & Self (1982), The volcanic explosivity index (VEI): An estimate of explosive magnitude for historical volcanism ↗

      Hearing the catalogue

      Sonification means representing data with sound. Each of the 9,906 Holocene eruption records in the saved GVP catalogue plays a drum or cymbal hit. The 7,719 records with a known VEI also play a quiet piano note. The 2,187 records with an unknown VEI play only a tap on the snare drum's rim. The four records without matched coordinates play equally through both speakers but have no map marker. These are musical sounds chosen to represent the data, not recordings of real eruptions.

      • Which drum or cymbal plays: VEI 0 uses closed hi-hat cymbals; 1, a bass drum (kick); 2, a snare drum; 3, a higher tom drum; 4, a lower tom drum; 5, a ride cymbal; 6, a crash cymbal; and 7-8, a China cymbal. Unknown VEI uses a drum-rim tap, a separate sound from VEI 0.
      • How high or low the piano note is: higher VEI produces a lower note that lasts longer. VEI 0-8 uses the notes D5, B4, A4, F#4, E4, D4, B3, A3 and F#3, in that order. Note duration is 0.24 + 0.065 x VEI seconds. These notes are musical choices, not the natural frequencies of eruptions.
      • Left and right speakers: longitude sets the speaker balance: western longitudes lean left and eastern longitudes lean right. Moving the map does not move the sound. The balance is longitude / 180, capped at -0.8 and +0.8 so sounds are not placed entirely in one speaker.
      • How gently piano notes begin: a start date specified to the day gives a 3-millisecond fade-in; month-only dates give 6 milliseconds; year-only dates give 12 milliseconds. Less precise dates therefore have a slightly softer beginning. This describes date precision, not eruption duration. Latitude and tectonic setting do not change the sound.
      • Volume: the piano is quieter than the drums, calibrated at 11 dB below them in the reference recording. When sounds overlap, their combined volume is reduced and limited to help prevent distortion. Loudness does not measure eruption energy or eruption count. No records are skipped to limit the number of sounds, although overlapping sounds can be hard to hear separately.
      • When sounds play: recorded eruption start dates determine the timing. At 1x, the default calendar playback lasts about three minutes, with different historical periods sped up by different amounts. Changing speed changes the spacing between sounds, not how high or low the notes are. MP4 exports always use the 1x calendar timing, regardless of the selected playback speed or mode. Event rhythm mode uses different timing. There is no added backing beat, random drum flourish or repeating melody. Records assigned the same start time play together.
      • What repeated sounds mean: dates known only to the year are placed at midyear by convention. Repeated VEI values, estimated dates, incomplete historical records and different playback rates can create audible patterns. A repeated sequence alone is not evidence of a volcanic cycle. Each sound represents a recorded eruption start, not every day that an eruption remained active.
